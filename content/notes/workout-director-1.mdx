This note describes how Workout Director counts reps from the camera on the iPhone, and which data leaves the device. It walks through the system from the camera and pose model up to the voice coach and the backend on AWS.

Last updated: 2 November 2026. The section on reconnecting now also describes the slower background retries that continue after the first 150 seconds, and that since version 1.0.66 the app and the coach say nothing about a lost connection.

## Background

Workout Director is an iPhone and iPad training app, published on the App Store by Shelgaon Ltd as "Workout Director - Gym & Home". The phone is propped up, Start is pressed, and the camera counts reps while a voice coach talks through the session.

The app uses the front camera by default. The back camera can be selected to film someone else.

I wrote the SwiftUI app and its TypeScript backend on AWS Lambda, and I ran the TestFlight and App Store releases. This is the first note in a series on how the app is built.

## The on-device boundary

One decision shaped most of the others: the camera feed stays on the phone. Video is never recorded, saved or uploaded.

A rep counter that streams video to a server would be easier in some ways, because it could run bigger models and fix counting bugs in one place. It would also mean sending footage of someone's body, often filmed at home, to a server, and every rep would depend on the network.

Keeping the camera local allows a privacy statement that can be made plainly in the App Store listing. It also splits the app into two parts.

The phone owns everything that has to happen in real time: pose tracking, counting, timers and cues. The network is used only for things a workout can continue without: syncing the log, the trainer chat and the voice coach.

### Pose tracking

Google's MediaPipe Pose Landmarker runs on the device with the BlazePose Full model. The model file, `pose_landmarker_full.task`, ships inside the app, so nothing is downloaded at run time and no account is needed.

The landmarker runs in MediaPipe's live-stream running mode and tracks one person. A frame that arrives while the previous one is still being processed is dropped.

If MediaPipe cannot start, or runs below 12 frames a second, that workout switches to Apple's Vision body pose (`VNDetectHumanBodyPoseRequest`) and keeps the count.

### The rep counter

The rep counter sits on top of the pose stream and counts squats, push-ups and more from the camera. Planks and other holds are timed sets instead, shown as the time left and a bar.

### Feedback

The count is shown large on screen. Short prerecorded clips in the coach's voice say each number out loud.

### The local log and Apple Health

Sets are stored on the phone with SwiftData. A finished workout is saved to Apple Health as one workout, written with HealthKit's `HKWorkoutBuilder` as a traditional strength training workout.

If the user allows it, heart rate and active calories are read from Apple Health for the set summary.

### Signed-out and manual use

None of the on-device layer needs an account. Camera workouts work signed out.

If camera access is declined, the app falls back to manual counting.

## Rules that keep the count accurate

Most of the work in counting reps from a pose stream is not detecting the movement. It is not counting the things that are not reps.

The App Store notes for version 1.0.86 list what was tightened: half reps, walking, standing up and getting into position no longer add reps, and counting keeps going for the whole workout.

Inside the counter, four rules do most of that work:

- **Each move has a shape.** A squat only counts if the hips go down, the legs move together and the feet stay put. A push-up needs the hands planted below the shoulders. The angle alone cannot tell a stride or a knee lift from a squat.
- **Some moves need a posture first.** A row, a press, a curl and a pull-up are all an elbow bending, and so is talking with your hands. A row only counts after the person has been bent over and still for a moment, a pull-up once they are hanging, and a bench press once they are lying back.
- **The first rep waits for a second one.** On squats, curls, presses, rows and deadlifts, a first rep is held for up to 8 seconds and only counts when another follows. A single bend while talking or tidying up never becomes a set.
- **The range is personal.** The first counted rep records the person's range, and later reps are judged against a share of it, never easier than a fixed floor.

Two more mechanisms sit around the counter rather than inside it:

- **Framing before the clock starts.** A short "3, 2, 1, Go" puts the person in position before the clock starts. During the set the screen says what to do (Move back, Come closer, Step into frame), and a setup check before the first set shows the one fix needed if something is off.
- **A manual path.** If the camera cannot see the person, minus and plus stay one tap away. With the voice coach on, the count can also be corrected by saying something like "That was 10".

## Feature flags and shadow mode

Changes to counting were the riskiest changes in the app, because a wrong count is the first thing a user notices. They ship behind feature flags.

Camera counting has its own flag, and MediaPipe has a switch that sends everyone back to Apple's Vision. The internal team gets a pose debug overlay that only the server can turn on.

Individual behaviours, such as asking for a count confirmation only when the counter is uncertain, can be turned off from the server. That does not require a new build to get through App Review.

I also trained a small rep classifier: gradient-boosted trees on pose features, run on the phone with Core ML. It ships in the app, but it only runs for the internal team, behind a server flag, in shadow mode.

The classifier scores every candidate rep and never changes the count, so it can be compared with the live counter on real phones and real sets. The pose model itself is Google's; I have not trained my own.

## The network layer: the voice coach

The voice coach is the only feature that needs the network during a set, so it is where the boundary is drawn most carefully.

### The call

The phone opens a WebRTC call to a realtime voice model, using an `RTCPeerConnection` from the WebRTC framework. Audio goes straight from the phone to the voice service.

When the call opens, the backend gives the model the user's profile, plan and recent workouts. During the workout the model gets short text notes about the state of the set over the WebRTC data channel (`RTCDataChannel`), and, if Apple Health is on, the set's heart rate and calories.

The model never gets video frames.

### Session setup and the client secret

The provider key never reaches the phone. The app asks the backend for a session.

A Lambda function checks the feature flags, the user's access and their limits, reads the key from AWS Secrets Manager and mints a short-lived client secret for the call. The secret is only needed to open a call; a call that is already open keeps going.

### Reconnecting and the speech fallback

If the connection drops, the app mints a new secret and reconnects quietly. The waits between tries are 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 seconds, then 30 seconds, each with some jitter. This first round ends once the total would pass 150 seconds.

After that, the app keeps trying in the background for the rest of the workout. It waits 10, 20, 40, 80 and 160 seconds, then 300 seconds between each later try, and it tries again about a second after the network comes back. Since version 1.0.66 all of this happens quietly. The app shows no reconnecting message and the coach does not announce that it is back. The new call is told not to greet or repeat the last cue, and it is given the last four lines of the conversation. The camera keeps counting reps and the counts keep playing on the phone the whole time.

While the coach is away, the app's own microphone takes over with Apple's speech recognition (`SFSpeechRecognizer`). Recognition runs on the device where the phone supports it, through `requiresOnDeviceRecognition`.

Workout commands such as "done" or "take a break" run on the phone. Anything else goes to the trainer as text, and the reply is spoken.

### When the coach speaks

Counts, timers and cues are handled on the phone, so the model speaks rarely. It speaks at the start, at each new exercise, at the end of each set, at the end of each exercise and of the workout, and always if the user mentions pain.

During rest it speaks once, if there are at least 20 seconds left. By default it gets one line at most in the middle of a set, for form, a stall or leaving the frame.

### Tool calls

The model asks and the app acts. When the user says "rest for 90 seconds" or "swap this, my shoulder hurts", the model replies with a tool call rather than changing anything itself.

These are some of the workout tools:

```text
log_set          correct_reps     next_exercise    previous_exercise
skip_exercise    swap_exercise    start_rest       extend_rest
skip_rest        pause_workout    resume_workout   change_target
add_set          remove_set       get_workout_status
get_history      get_set_summary  set_coach_style  mute_counts
end_workout
```

The phone runs the tool against its own workout state and shows a small chip with Undo. Larger edits wait for a Confirm, ending the workout waits for a spoken yes, and mentioning pain stops the exercise and offers a swap.

The workout state lives on the phone, and the model can only request changes to it.

## The backend on AWS

### Compute and API

The backend is TypeScript on AWS Lambda, on the Node.js 22 runtime and arm64, behind an API Gateway HTTP API and CloudFront. It is defined with the AWS CDK, and each function is bundled with the CDK's `NodejsFunction` construct.

It is split into functions by job: one for profile, sessions and sync; one for the trainer and voice tokens; one for sign-in; one for operations; and one for subscription webhooks.

### Data

App data is in six DynamoDB tables in on-demand capacity mode, with point-in-time recovery. The tables are encrypted with a customer managed AWS KMS key.

### Sign-in

Sign-in uses Better Auth, with Sign in with Apple, Google and email codes. Better Auth runs as a Hono app in its own Lambda function, and its data is in Postgres on Neon.

The other functions verify a short-lived RS256 JWT themselves with the `jose` library, against Better Auth's JWKS.

### Sync on unreliable connections

Two details matter for a phone that is often offline in a gym. Workout uploads and sync honour an `Idempotency-Key` header and keep the response for an hour, so a retried upload does not create a duplicate workout.

Sync does a last-write-wins merge in batches, so the same log can follow the user to another device. A later part covers the data model.
